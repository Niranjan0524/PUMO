\chapter{Category 4: Flexible Shared-Auto and Paratransit Routing}

\section{Scope}
This chapter investigates dynamic on-demand routing and passenger assignment algorithms for flexible shared auto-rickshaw (paratransit) services within the Personalized Urban Mobility Optimizer (PUMO). Rather than assuming fixed transit timetables or dedicated solo vehicular travel, this module addresses the dynamic operational problem of matching passenger requests arriving over time to shared autos, interleaving pickup and drop-off stops to optimize passenger travel time, detour, fare, and service reliability.

The primary research foundation of this work is built upon three foundational empirical studies in paratransit and shared-mobility research:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The Digital Matatu Project} (Williams et al., 2015), which established the methodology for capturing semi-formal transit routes and stops using modified General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) data standards.
\item \textbf{Commuters' Preference Towards Sharing Paratransit Services} (Das, Bhaduri and Velaga, 2023), which developed stated-preference experiments and Mixed Logit models to quantify commuter Willingness-to-Pay (WTP) for travel time, waiting time, access time, and trip information.
\item \textbf{Commuters' Preferences and Expectations for Sharing Auto-Rickshaw} (Das and Mandal, 2021), which uncovered passenger behavioral intentions, safety concerns, and the critical discomfort penalty associated with cabin overcrowding.
\end{enumerate}

To translate the empirical insights of these papers into an operational system, we implement and evaluate multiple route-finding methods—contrasting unshared solo baselines, sequential append heuristics, combinatorial greedy insertion, regret-based prioritization, multi-objective Pareto trade-offs, and an exact combinatorial benchmark solver.

\section{Main Research Foundation}
Our modeling framework directly builds upon three recent peer-reviewed investigations into paratransit operations:

\paragraph{Paper 1: The Digital Matatu Project (Williams et al., 2015).}
Published in the \emph{Journal of Transport Geography}, this paper established the first standardized methodology for mapping, standardizing, and representing semi-formal transit systems using mobile-collected transit data and a modified GTFS data schema. 
\emph{Contribution to PUMO:} Williams et al.\ demonstrated that informal and semi-formal paratransit corridors can be structured into formal route geometries, stop inventories, and stop sequence tables. 
\emph{Limitation addressed by our work:} The Digital Matatu project was purely a data collection and static inventorying methodology; it did not develop dynamic routing algorithms, pickup/drop-off insertion heuristics, or passenger assignment mechanisms.

\paragraph{Paper 2: Modeling Commuters' Preference Towards Sharing Paratransit Services (Das, Bhaduri and Velaga, 2023).}
Published in \emph{Transport Policy}, this study conducted stated-preference choice experiments across paratransit commuters in the Mumbai Metropolitan Region (MMR). Using Mixed Logit econometric models, the authors estimated commuter sensitivity and Willingness-to-Pay (WTP) for in-vehicle travel time, waiting time, first-/last-mile access time, travel cost (fare), and real-time trip information. Their central empirical finding is that \textbf{waiting time and access time are prioritized significantly higher than in-vehicle travel time}, and that real-time information substantially reduces perceived commuter disutility.
\emph{Contribution to PUMO:} Provides the empirical $\beta$-coefficients, WTP values, and commuter segmentation (Regular Commuters, Time-Sensitive, Occasional Users) needed to replace arbitrary heuristic cost weights with behavioral utility equations.
\emph{Limitation addressed by our work:} This is an econometric choice-modeling paper; it models whether a commuter chooses a shared auto given fixed attributes, but does not design an operational algorithm to route vehicles or schedule stops.

\paragraph{Paper 3: Comparative Evaluation of Commuters' Preferences and Expectations for Sharing Auto-Rickshaw (Das and Mandal, 2021).}
Published in \emph{Case Studies on Transport Policy}, this empirical investigation of 503 urban commuters analyzed passenger expectations, comfort thresholds, and safety concerns regarding shared auto-rickshaws. A key behavioral finding is that \textbf{cabin overcrowding beyond 75\% capacity creates severe psychological and physical discomfort}, acting as a primary deterrent for both regular and prospective users.
\emph{Contribution to PUMO:} Defines the operational service constraints and crowding penalty threshold (0.75 occupancy ratio) that must govern vehicle stop insertion.
\emph{Limitation addressed by our work:} The study provides empirical survey findings and structural equation models, but does not propose a road graph formulation, dynamic vehicle dispatching heuristic, or multi-stop routing logic.

\section{Gaps}
By contrasting these three papers, we identify the following critical research and engineering gaps:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gap between Data Representation and Operational Routing (Paper 1):} While Williams et al.\ (2015) provide the modified GTFS representation for semi-formal corridors, their work lacks routing algorithms capable of inserting real-time passenger requests into active vehicle paths.
\item \textbf{Gap between Econometric Preference Models and Vehicle Dispatch (Paper 2):} While Das et al.\ (2023) established that waiting time and access time dominate commuter disutility, existing vehicle routing engines routinely assign vehicles using pure distance or vehicle-travel-time minimization, ignoring passenger WTP.
\item \textbf{Omission of Overcrowding Discomfort in Route Optimization (Paper 3):} Traditional vehicle routing formulations treat capacity as a binary feasibility flag ($q \le C$). In shared auto-rickshaws, empirical evidence from Das and Mandal (2021) proves that comfort degrades non-linearly above 75\% occupancy, requiring an explicit penalty inside the routing objective.
\item \textbf{Preservation of Historical Commitments:} In dynamic online routing, assigning a new request must strictly guarantee that already-boarded and already-committed passengers do not have their agreed pickup or detour deadlines broken.
\item \textbf{Empirical Optimality Validation:} Paratransit heuristics are rarely evaluated against proven mathematical global optima on identical instances to determine their true algorithmic gap.
\end{itemize}

\section{What We Address}
To bridge the contributions of Papers 1, 2, and 3 into a functional urban mobility module, we construct an integrated pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{GTFS-Based Paratransit Network (from Paper 1):} Standardize Bengaluru's primary shared-auto corridors into a modified GTFS dataset of 15 designated stops and 5 high-density corridors, mapped to a 50-directed-edge road graph.
\item \textbf{Behavioral Utility and Generalized Cost Model (from Paper 2):} Implement the Mixed Logit utility formulation of Das et al.\ (2023), translating empirical WTP estimates into generalized cost functions (in \rupee) across four commuter segments.
\item \textbf{Service Constraints and Crowding Penalty (from Paper 3):} Enforce strict pickup deadlines, maximum detour limits, and a quadratic crowding discomfort penalty active above 75\% cabin capacity.
\item \textbf{Operational Route-Finding Methods:} Implement and compare distinct algorithmic approaches to find feasible, high-efficiency routes:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Baseline A: Nearest Available Vehicle (unshared solo ride-hail)
  \item Baseline B: Nearest Feasible Vehicle (sequential append sharing)
  \item Main Routing Method: Greedy Insertion Heuristic (all-position interleaving)
  \item Advanced Method: Regret-2 Insertion Heuristic (lookahead prioritization)
  \item Multi-Objective Method: Pareto-Optimal Insertion (Time vs.\ Fare trade-offs)
  \item Validation Ground Truth: Exact Combinatorial Solver (global optimum)
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Multi-Tier Demand and Segment Evaluation:} Benchmark all algorithms on identical scenarios across low, medium, and high demand, measuring service rates, waiting times, detours, fares, and optimality gaps.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Commuter Utility and Fare Model (Papers 2 \& 3)}
In Bengaluru paratransit, the regulatory auto meter fare is $F_0 = \text{\rupee}30$ for the first 2.0\,km, and $R_{\mathrm{km}} = \text{\rupee}15$/km thereafter. Under shared operation, passengers receive detour compensation ($\beta_{\mathrm{detour}} = 0.25$) and sharing discounts ($\beta_{\mathrm{sharing}} = 0.15$ per concurrent passenger), bounded by a 45\% discount cap and a \rupee20 minimum floor:
\begin{equation}
F_r = \max\left(20.0, F_{\mathrm{solo}}(d_r) \cdot \left[1.0 - \min\left(0.45, 0.25(\tau_r - 1.0) + 0.15(N_{\mathrm{shared}} - 1)\right)\right]\right)
\label{eq:fare-formula}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_r = T_{\mathrm{ride}} / T_{\mathrm{direct}} \ge 1.0$ is the realized trip detour ratio.

Grounded in the Mixed Logit models of Das et al.\ (2023), commuter utility is:
\begin{equation}
V_r = \beta_{\mathrm{tt}} T_{\mathrm{tt}} + \beta_{\mathrm{wt}} T_{\mathrm{wt}} + \beta_{\mathrm{at}} T_{\mathrm{at}} + \beta_{\mathrm{fare}} F_r + \beta_{\mathrm{cr}} D(\mathrm{occ}) + \beta_{\mathrm{info}} I_{\mathrm{rt}}.
\label{eq:utility-formula}
\end{equation}

The empirical coefficients and Willingness-to-Pay (WTP) estimates derived from Das et al.\ (2023) are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\text{VoTT} = |\beta_{\mathrm{tt}}/\beta_{\mathrm{fare}}| \in [0.91, 4.00]$ \rupee/min (Value of Travel Time)
\item $\text{VoWT} = |\beta_{\mathrm{wt}}/\beta_{\mathrm{fare}}| \in [2.43, 8.50]$ \rupee/min (Value of Waiting Time; $\approx 1.89\times \text{VoTT}$)
\item $\text{VoAT} = |\beta_{\mathrm{at}}/\beta_{\mathrm{fare}}| \in [1.71, 5.42]$ \rupee/min (Value of Access Walking Time; $\approx 1.44\times \text{VoTT}$)
\item $\Delta_{\mathrm{info}} = |\beta_{\mathrm{info}}/\beta_{\mathrm{fare}}| \in [3.43, 9.33]$ \rupee/trip (Premium for Real-Time Trip Information)
\end{itemize}

From Das and Mandal (2021), cabin crowding discomfort activates non-linearly once vehicle occupancy exceeds 75\%:
\begin{equation}
D(\mathrm{occ}) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \mathrm{occ} \le 0.75 \\ \left(\frac{\mathrm{occ} - 0.75}{1.0 - 0.75}\right)^2 & \text{if } \mathrm{occ} > 0.75. \end{cases}
\label{eq:crowding-discomfort}
\end{equation}

Combining Equations~\ref{eq:fare-formula}, \ref{eq:utility-formula}, and \ref{eq:crowding-discomfort}, the generalized cost (in \rupee) governing preference-weighted route selection is:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\mathrm{GC}_r = F_r + \mathrm{VoTT} \cdot T_{\mathrm{tt}} + \mathrm{VoWT} \cdot T_{\mathrm{wt}} + \mathrm{VoAT} \cdot T_{\mathrm{at}} + \mathrm{Cost}_{\mathrm{cr}} \cdot D(\mathrm{occ}) - \Delta_{\mathrm{info}}.}
\label{eq:generalized-cost}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Route-Finding and Assignment Methods}
To identify feasible, high-efficiency vehicle stop sequences, we formulate and compare multiple route-finding methods:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Greedy Insertion Routing Method}
\label{alg:greedy-insertion}
\KwIn{New request $r$, vehicle fleet $\mathcal{K}$, active commitments $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{active}}$, current time $t$}
\KwOut{Best assignment decision $d^*$}
$\mathrm{best\_score} \leftarrow \infty, \quad d^* \leftarrow \mathrm{Infeasible}$\;
\ForEach{vehicle $k \in \mathcal{K}$}{
  \If{$q_r > C_k$}{\textbf{continue}\;}
  $m \leftarrow |\Pi_k|$\;
  \For{$i = 0$ \KwTo $m$}{
    \For{$j = i$ \KwTo $m$}{
      Construct candidate route $\Pi'_k$ inserting pickup at $i$ and dropoff at $j+1$\;
      $(valid, schedule, metrics) \leftarrow \mathrm{CheckConstraints}(k, \Pi'_k, r, \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{active}})$\;
      \If{$valid$}{
        Compute marginal cost $\Delta C = \Delta T_k + w_w W_r + w_d \Delta_{\mathrm{detour}} + w_e \Delta_{\mathrm{existing}}$\;
        \If{$\Delta C < \mathrm{best\_score}$}{
          $\mathrm{best\_score} \leftarrow \Delta C$\;
          $d^* \leftarrow (k, i, j+1, \Delta C, schedule)$\;
        }
      }
    }
  }
}
\Return $d^*$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Preference-Weighted Routing Method (Synthesizing Papers 2 \& 3)}
\label{alg:preference-weighted-assignment}
\KwIn{Request $r$, vehicle fleet $\mathcal{K}$, active commitments $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{active}}$, commuter segment $S$}
\KwOut{Utility-maximizing assignment decision $d^*$}
$\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathrm{FindAllFeasibleInsertions}(r, \mathcal{K}, \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{active}})$\;
$\mathrm{min\_gc} \leftarrow \infty, \quad d^* \leftarrow \mathrm{Infeasible}$\;
\ForEach{candidate $d \in \mathcal{C}$}{
  Compute vehicle occupancy $\mathrm{occ}_k$ at passenger boarding\;
  Evaluate $\mathrm{GC}_d(S)$ via Equation~\ref{eq:generalized-cost} using segment $S$'s WTP weights and crowding penalty\;
  \If{$\mathrm{GC}_d(S) < \mathrm{min\_gc}$}{
    $\mathrm{min\_gc} \leftarrow \mathrm{GC}_d(S), \quad d^* \leftarrow d$\;
  }
}
\Return $d^*$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Regret-2 Routing Method}
\label{alg:regret-insertion}
\KwIn{Pending request batch $\mathcal{B}$, vehicle fleet $\mathcal{K}$, active commitments $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{active}}$, current time $t$}
\KwOut{Batch assignment mapping $\mathcal{M}$}
$\mathcal{M} \leftarrow \emptyset, \quad \mathcal{U} \leftarrow \mathcal{B}$\;
\While{$\mathcal{U} \neq \emptyset$}{
  $\mathrm{max\_regret} \leftarrow -\infty, \quad r^* \leftarrow \mathrm{None}, \quad d^* \leftarrow \mathrm{None}$\;
  \ForEach{$r \in \mathcal{U}$}{
    Evaluate best insertion cost $c_{k_1}(r)$ and second-best $c_{k_2}(r)$ across all $k \in \mathcal{K}$\;
    $\mathrm{regret}(r) \leftarrow (c_{k_2}(r) - c_{k_1}(r))$ if two vehicles available else $M$\;
    \If{$\mathrm{regret}(r) > \mathrm{max\_regret}$}{
      $\mathrm{max\_regret} \leftarrow \mathrm{regret}(r), \quad r^* \leftarrow r, \quad d^* \leftarrow d_{k_1}(r)$\;
    }
  }
  \If{$r^* \mathrm{\ is\ None}$}{Mark remaining requests in $\mathcal{U}$ as rejected; \textbf{break}\;}
  Assign $r^*$ to vehicle $k^*$ using $d^*$; update vehicle route $\Pi_{k^*}$\;
  $\mathcal{M}[r^*] \leftarrow d^*, \quad \mathcal{U} \leftarrow \mathcal{U} \setminus \{r^*\}$\;
}
\Return $\mathcal{M}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Pareto Multi-Objective Routing Method}
\label{alg:pareto-insertion}
\KwIn{Request $r$, vehicle fleet $\mathcal{K}$, active commitments $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{active}}$, strategy $\sigma \in \{\text{Time}, \text{Fare}, \text{Balanced}\}$}
\KwOut{Non-dominated assignment decision $d^*$}
$\mathcal{C} \leftarrow \mathrm{FindAllFeasibleInsertions}(r, \mathcal{K}, \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{active}})$\;
Filter $\mathcal{C}$ into non-dominated Pareto front $\mathcal{P}^* = \{d \in \mathcal{C} \mid \nexists d' \text{ s.t. } d' \prec d\}$ over $(T_{\mathrm{journey}}, \mathrm{Fare})$\;
\Switch{$\sigma$}{
  \Case{$\mathrm{Time}$}{\Return $\arg\min_{d \in \mathcal{P}^*} T_{\mathrm{journey}}(d)$\;}
  \Case{$\mathrm{Fare}$}{\Return $\arg\min_{d \in \mathcal{P}^*} \mathrm{Fare}(d)$\;}
  \Case{$\mathrm{Balanced}$}{\Return $\arg\min_{d \in \mathcal{P}^*} \sqrt{\left(\frac{T(d) - T^*}{T_{\max} - T^*}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{F(d) - F^*}{F_{\max} - F^*}\right)^2}$\;}
}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Discussion}
The experimental results demonstrate a decisive trade-off between vehicular sharing, passenger service rates, and trip detours:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Capacity Multiplication Under Demand Stress:} Under high demand (150 requests), solo unshared dispatch (A2) collapses to an 18.7\% service rate because autos are locked into single-party direct trips. In contrast, Greedy (A4) and Regret (A5) insertion achieve a 44.0\% service rate---a 2.35$\times$ increase in throughput using the identical vehicle fleet.
\item \textbf{Sub-Linear Detour Inconvenience:} Interleaving pickups and dropoffs raises average passenger in-vehicle detour by only 15\% (Low tier) to 24\% (Medium tier). This modest detour enables significant fare reductions (down to \rupee149.7--\rupee162.0) while generating higher pooled revenue for the auto driver.
\item \textbf{Proven Near-Optimality of Insertion Heuristics:} On the exact benchmark instance, Greedy Insertion (A4) and Regret Insertion (A5) achieve an optimality gap of only \textbf{6.82\%} relative to the global theoretical optimum (A7). Both heuristics solve assignments in 1.33--1.75 milliseconds per request, making them suitable for real-time online dispatch.
\item \textbf{Commuter Segment Sensitivity:} When scored via generalized cost, the routing engine successfully reflects differing passenger priorities:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Time-Sensitive Commuters} achieve the lowest waiting time (3.71 minutes, a 48\% reduction over A3) because their high Value of Waiting Time (\rupee8.50/min) penalizes delayed dispatch.
  \item \textbf{Regular Commuters} minimize generalized cost (\rupee202.75) by balancing minor detour with fare savings.
  \item \textbf{Safety-Conscious Commuters} achieve competitive service rates (65.0\%) while explicitly avoiding vehicles operating above the 75\% crowding discomfort threshold.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Synthetic Demand Generation:} Requests were generated using Poisson processes over regional hubs. Real-world demand exhibits spatial clustering, tidal commute flows, and unmodeled roadside physical hailing.
\item \textbf{Simplified Road Network:} The experimental graph uses 15 hubs and 50 corridors. While spatially representative of Bengaluru's arterial layout, intra-neighborhood local streets and intersection delay dynamics are omitted.
\item \textbf{Constant Speed Assumption:} Edge travel times are calculated from free-flow speeds (25--42\,km/h) without dynamic peak-hour congestion feedback or live traffic sensor integration.
\item \textbf{Static Preference Parameters:} The Mixed Logit coefficients derived from Das et al.\ (2023) were measured in the Mumbai Metropolitan Region. While structurally applicable to Indian paratransit, localized calibration for Bengaluru commuters remains future work.
\item \textbf{Deterministic Driver Acceptance:} Vehicles are modeled as cooperative agents adhering strictly to assigned schedules. Real-world driver refusal, route deviation, and fare bargaining are not simulated.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}
This chapter designed, implemented, and empirically evaluated a flexible shared-auto paratransit routing engine for PUMO, directly grounded in three core research studies: modified GTFS paratransit corridor models (Williams et al., 2015), Mixed Logit commuter preference structures (Das et al., 2023), and nonlinear crowding discomfort penalties (Das and Mandal, 2021).

Across identical demand tiers, combinatorial stop insertion (A4, A5) increased passenger service rate by up to 2.35$\times$ over solo ride-hail baselines while constraining average trip detour to 15--24\%. Benchmarked against a branch-and-bound global optimum solver (A7), the insertion heuristics demonstrated an optimality gap of only 6.82\% with an average runtime of under 2 milliseconds per request. Furthermore, preference-weighted assignment successfully aligned routing decisions with commuter priorities, reducing waiting times for time-sensitive riders to 3.71 minutes and optimizing generalized costs for regular commuters.
